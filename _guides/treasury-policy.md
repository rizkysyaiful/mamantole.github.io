If your company is thinking about Bitcoin, the first document you need is not a wallet. It is a policy.

A policy turns an opinion into a decision the board can approve, an auditor can test and a successor can follow. This guide walks through what to put in it.

## Who this is for

CFOs, treasurers, finance controllers and anyone who will draft the paper that goes to the board.

## Why a written policy comes first

Have you ever watched a treasury decision get made in a hallway conversation? It works until the person who made it leaves.

A policy answers four things before anything is bought: why, how much, who decides and how we stop. Banks write these down for every risk they take, and your company can too.

## The seven sections

### 1. Purpose and scope

State in two or three sentences **what risk this covers**. For example: "to hold a limited reserve that is not exposed to the issuance of any single currency."

Say what it is not for. For example: not for operating cash, not for speculation, not for short-term trading. A clear "not for" protects the finance team later.

### 2. Allocation

Set a **cap as a share of reserves**, and a floor for liquid cash that must remain. Name the reserves it applies to.

Decide the cap with your own numbers, not someone else's. A useful test is: if the position fell by half, could we still pay salaries and suppliers for the planning period without selling it?

### 3. Buying method

Say **how** you will build the position. A common choice is gradual purchases on a schedule, so no single day carries the decision. Name the licensed venues you may use, and the limits per transaction.

### 4. Custody

Pick the custody model and write it down: self-custody with a signing quorum, a regulated custodian, or a mix. List who holds what, where backups sit, and how recovery works.

The [custody guide]({{ '/guides/custody-options/' | relative_url }}) compares the options.

### 5. Authority and approvals

Spell out who may **propose**, **approve** and **execute**, and who can say stop. Separate the roles so no one person can do all three.

A simple matrix works: action in one column, role in the next, and a limit in the last.

### 6. Accounting and reporting

Say which accounting treatment you expect to apply, subject to your auditor, and how often the board sees the position. Include the price source you will use for valuation.

The [accounting and audit guide]({{ '/guides/accounting-and-audit/' | relative_url }}) covers what auditors ask.

### 7. Review and exit

Set a review date, and write the **triggers** that force an early review: the position falling below or rising above set levels, a change in law, a custody incident, or a change in the company's circumstances.

Also write what you will do in each case: pause purchases, rebalance, or reduce.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the policy after the first purchase.
- A cap with no reason behind it.
- No named owner for each custody key.
- Approval by one person.
- No review date, so the policy never changes.
- Copying another company's numbers.

## A one-page template

Use these headings as the skeleton and fill each with two or three sentences:

1. Purpose and what it is not for
2. Allocation cap and liquidity floor
3. Buying method and approved venues
4. Custody model, roles and recovery
5. Authority matrix
6. Accounting, valuation and board reporting
7. Review date and triggers

## The bottom line

A good policy is short, specific and honest about the trade-offs. If a director can read it in five minutes and say what the company will do in a bad month, it is doing its job.

Legal, tax and accounting conclusions stay with your own advisers. Bring this draft to them.

## Checklist

- Purpose written, with what it is not for
- Cap and liquidity floor set from your own cash flows
- Buying method and venues approved
- Custody model, roles and recovery written
- Authority matrix with separated roles
- Accounting treatment discussed with the auditor
- Review date and triggers set
