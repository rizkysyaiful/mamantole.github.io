Holding Bitcoin does not break an audit. Surprising the auditor does.

This guide shows what auditors tend to ask, and the evidence a finance team can prepare ahead of time. It does not give accounting or tax conclusions. Those belong to your auditor and advisers.

## Who this is for

Finance controllers, CFOs and audit committee members.

## Start with a conversation, not a ledger

Have you told your auditor yet? If the answer is no, do that first.

Auditors do better with early notice. Share your draft policy, explain why you hold the asset, and ask which evidence they will want. You will avoid most surprises.

## Which standard applies?

It depends on where you report, and on why you hold the asset. Two reference points:

- **IFRS.** The IFRS Interpretations Committee [considered crypto holdings in 2019](https://www.ifrs.org/projects/completed-projects/2019/holdings-of-cryptocurrencies/). Depending on why they are held, [IAS 2 (Inventories)](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-2-inventories/) or [IAS 38 (Intangible Assets)](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-38-intangible-assets/) may be relevant.
- **US GAAP.** [FASB's ASU 2023-08](https://www.fasb.org/) introduced fair value measurement for certain crypto assets.

Your local standards may differ. Treat this as the list of documents to read with your auditor.

## What auditors usually ask

Think of five steps, in order.

### 1. Control

Can you show the company controls the keys? Prepare a message signed from each relevant wallet, produced in front of someone independent, and keep a record of when and by whom.

### 2. Existence

Is the balance real? The Bitcoin ledger is public, so the auditor can check balances independently. Give them a list of addresses and the dates they should check.

### 3. Valuation

Which price will you use, and at what time? Pick **one source and one cut-off time** and write it down. Using the same method every period matters more than which one you choose.

### 4. Treatment

Which accounting standard applies, and why? Prepare a short paper stating the reason you hold the asset and the treatment you propose. Let your auditor challenge it.

### 5. Disclosure

What do your notes say about the holding, its risks and any restrictions? Include the custody arrangement, because third-party custody can raise questions about who legally holds the asset.

## The evidence pack

Prepare these before year end, not during the audit:

1. List of wallet addresses and the people who control each.
2. Signed messages proving control.
3. Custody agreement or written self-custody procedures.
4. Approval records for each transaction.
5. Price-source policy and the valuation each period.
6. Reconciliation of holdings to the ledger.

The [treasury policy guide]({{ '/guides/treasury-policy/' | relative_url }}) lists where these fit.

## Controls auditors like to see

- **Separation of duties.** The person who proposes is not the person who approves.
- **A signing quorum,** so no single person can move funds.
- **Regular reconciliation** between your records and the public ledger.
- **Tested recovery,** with the date of the last drill.

## Common mistakes

- Telling the auditor after the first purchase.
- No signed proof of control.
- Changing the price source from period to period.
- Funds held by a third party with no written agreement.
- No record of who approved each move.

## The bottom line

Auditing Bitcoin is mostly documentation. If you can show control, existence, a consistent valuation method and a written policy, you have given your auditor what they need.

Ask them early. Let them tell you what evidence they prefer.

## Checklist

- Auditor informed and consulted before purchase
- Applicable standards identified with the auditor
- Signed proof of control prepared and dated
- Price source and cut-off time written down
- Custody agreement or procedures documented
- Reconciliation process set
- Disclosure wording drafted for review
