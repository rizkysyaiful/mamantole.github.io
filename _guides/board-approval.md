You believe in the idea. The board has not heard it yet. Between those two points sits a lot of work, and most of it is not persuasion.

This guide gives you a checklist for getting a Bitcoin treasury proposal in front of your board in a form they can decide on.

## Who this is for

CEOs, CFOs and company secretaries who will carry the proposal to the board.

## Think of it as five stages

Each stage ends with something written. If a stage has nothing written, it is not done.

## Stage 1: Brief

Start with the problem, not the asset. Write one page that answers: what is the risk to our reserves, how big is it, and what are the options?

Keep it in plain words. If a director cannot repeat it to a colleague, it is too complicated.

> What's in it for them? Why should this board spend its attention on this?

Answer that in the first paragraph.

## Stage 2: Educate

Most directors have heard headlines, not explanations. Plan a short [leadership session]({{ '/guides/leadership-session/' | relative_url }}) before the formal paper.

Let people ask the "silly" questions in a room without a vote. You will learn which worries are real.

## Stage 3: Draft the policy and the decision paper

Two documents, not one.

- **The policy.** The [treasury policy guide]({{ '/guides/treasury-policy/' | relative_url }}) shows seven sections. Keep it to one page.
- **The decision paper.** What you are asking the board to decide, the options considered, the risks, and the recommendation.

A decision paper should end with a request that can be answered yes or no. For example: "approve a pilot allocation of up to X% of reserves, under the attached policy, with review in six months."

## Stage 4: Pre-read and pre-wire

Send the papers early. Meet directors one at a time if you can. Ask each one: "What is your biggest worry?"

You will hear the same few concerns:

1. **Volatility.** Show the size cap and what a severe fall would do. See [the volatility myth]({{ '/myths/too-volatile-for-treasury/' | relative_url }}).
2. **Custody and loss.** Show the signing quorum and recovery drill. See [the keys myth]({{ '/myths/lose-the-keys-gone/' | relative_url }}).
3. **Accounting and audit.** Show you have talked to the auditor. See [the accounting myth]({{ '/myths/cannot-audit-or-account/' | relative_url }}).
4. **Regulation.** Show the checklist your lawyer has answered. See [the regulation myth]({{ '/myths/regulators-will-not-allow/' | relative_url }}).
5. **Reputation.** Be honest about it. Decide what you will say to staff, customers and press.

Where a director raises something new, add it to the paper.

## Stage 5: Decide

At the meeting, present for ten minutes and leave the rest for questions.

Do not defend the asset. Present the **trade-offs**. A board that can see the downside written down trusts the upside more.

Ask for a decision on the smallest sensible step. A first step with a review date is easier to approve than an open commitment.

## After the yes

- Record the resolution and the exact limits approved.
- Set the first review date now.
- Assign a named owner for each custody and approval role.
- Tell the auditor.

Do nothing with money until these are done.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with the asset instead of the problem.
- Surprising a director in the meeting.
- A paper that argues instead of presenting trade-offs.
- Asking for too much at once.
- No review date.

## The bottom line

Boards say yes to clear, limited, reviewable steps. They say no to surprises.

At the end of the day, a board is a group of people trying to protect the company. Give them what they need to do it well.

## Checklist

- One-page brief written in plain words
- Leadership session held
- One-page policy and a decision paper drafted
- Auditor and lawyer consulted
- Each director's main concern heard and answered
- A yes-or-no request with a limit written
- Review date and named owners set
