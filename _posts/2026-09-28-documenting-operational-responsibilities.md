## Follow one task from beginning to end

A process document can sound complete while still leaving people unsure what to do. One way to review it is to follow a single task through the proposed workflow: who starts it, who checks it, who records the outcome, and who handles an exception?

Write those steps in everyday language. A colleague who did not draft the document should be able to describe what happens next without guessing.

## Give each step an owner

A list of departments is less useful than a clear description of responsibilities. Identify the role that performs the task, the role that reviews it, and the person or group that resolves an unresolved question.

The aim is to make accountability understandable. If two teams both think the other owns a step, the document needs another pass.

## Make handovers visible

For every handover, describe the information passed along and how the receiving person knows the task is ready. A completed action and a documented action are not always the same thing.

Consider how someone joining the team would find the relevant records. Consistent names, an agreed location, and a clear version history make a document easier to use in practice.

## Walk through an exception

A walkthrough should include an ordinary day and a day when something does not go as expected. Ask what happens if a required person is unavailable or a record is incomplete. Record the questions that emerge instead of resolving them through improvisation.

These discussions are preparation for a reviewed operating process. They do not replace the technical or specialist review a particular system may require.

## Close with a review point

Assign an owner for maintaining the document and identify when it will be revisited. A responsibility map is useful only while it reflects how the team actually works.
