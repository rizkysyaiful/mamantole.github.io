## Your bank balance did not change. Your payroll did.

Open your bank statement. The number looks fine.

Now open your payroll and your supplier invoices from three years ago. Is the same amount of cash still buying the same amount of work?

## "Under control" is a statement about the rate, not the level

When officials say inflation is under control, they usually mean it is rising slowly. That is good news.

It does not mean prices are falling back. Prices stay at the new, higher level, and the next year's increase is added on top. The loss does not announce itself. It simply compounds.

## A worked example, not a forecast

Take a round number. Suppose prices rise 3% a year. That is an assumption for illustration, not a prediction for any country.

Five years later, the same cash buys about 14% less. Nothing was spent. Nothing was lost to a mistake. It just sat still.

For your own numbers, check the latest consumer price data from [Statistics Indonesia (BPS)](https://www.bps.go.id/) and [Bank Indonesia](https://www.bi.go.id/), or your own country's statistics office.

## But wait, does this mean "buy Bitcoin"?

No. And I want to be careful here.

Inflation is a reason to **look at idle cash**, not proof that any single answer is right. There are several ways a company can respond. It can move some cash into short-term deposits or government bills. It can shift part of its reserves into assets that tend to hold value. It can keep more of its pricing power by adjusting contracts.

Bitcoin is one option among these, with its own risks. Its price can fall sharply. We look at that honestly in [the volatility myth]({{ '/myths/too-volatile-for-treasury/' | relative_url }}).

## Separate the cash you need from the cash you wait on

Most companies hold three kinds of cash, even if they never name them.

1. **Operating cash.** Pays salaries and suppliers in the next few months. Keep it liquid and boring.
2. **Reserve cash.** A buffer for bad months. It should be safe and easy to reach.
3. **Idle cash.** Money with no job for years. This is where inflation does its quiet work.

The question is not "should we protect everything?" It is "how much idle cash do we really have, and what is a reasonable plan for it?"

## A better question

> If this cash is still idle in five years, what will it buy, and are we comfortable with that?

Ask your finance team to put a number on that answer. Then ask what the options cost, and what each one risks.

At the end of the day, your company is a group of people who need to be paid on time. Protecting that ability is the whole point.
