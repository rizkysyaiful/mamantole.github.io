## "Waste" is a judgement, not a measurement.

Bitcoin uses a lot of electricity. That is a fact. Whether the use is waste is a value judgement, and it deserves a better debate than a slogan.

Start with what the energy is actually doing.

## What is true in the myth

Mining uses real electricity at real scale. The [Cambridge Bitcoin Electricity Consumption Index](https://ccaf.io/cbnsi/cbeci) publishes estimates and updates them, and the figure is large. Some mining runs on fossil fuels. Some does not. The mix matters.

A company with energy or environmental commitments has to take this seriously. It is a fair question for a board.

## Where the myth breaks down

The energy is not an accident. It is the design.

<figure><img src="{{ 'images/figures/bitcoin-wastes-energy-1.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Four steps: electricity, hashing work, cost to attack, and a ledger anyone can verify." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Electricity becomes work. The work becomes a cost to attack the ledger. That cost is what lets strangers trust it without a middleman.</figcaption></figure>

Miners spend electricity to compete for the right to add a block. To rewrite history, an attacker would have to pay that cost again, faster than everyone else. That cost is the security. It is described in the [original paper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf) as proof-of-work.

So the honest question is not "does it use energy?" It does. The question is: **is the security worth the energy, and where does the energy come from?**

## Both sides of the debate

Critics point to the total consumption, to emissions where mining uses fossil fuels, and to the fact that other systems secure payments with less energy.

Supporters point to the security that cost buys, to miners that use stranded or surplus power, and to the fact that existing systems use energy too, in banks, branches and vaults. Comparing them fairly is hard, and both sides often cherry-pick.

I would not ask you to pick a side. I would ask you to use measured data.

## What to do with this

1. **Use a measured source.** Read the Cambridge index, not a headline.
2. **Ask about the mix.** What share is renewable, and how is it measured?
3. **Match it to your commitments.** If you report on emissions, ask your sustainability lead what holding the asset means for your reporting.
4. **Say what you do not know.** If the evidence is mixed, write that down.

## A better question

> What would we need to be comfortable with on energy, and can we get that evidence?

If the answer is a threshold, you can write it into the policy. If it is "never," that is a legitimate policy too.
