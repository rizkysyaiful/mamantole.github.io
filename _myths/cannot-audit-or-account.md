## Can your auditor follow the trail?

That is the real question behind "we can't audit it." Not "is there a standard?" but "will the person who signs our accounts be able to verify what we say we hold?"

The answer is usually yes, if you prepare for it.

## What is true in the myth

Accounting for Bitcoin is not as simple as accounting for a bank balance. Standards differ by country, and they are still developing. Some auditors have limited experience with it, and some ask for more evidence than they would for cash.

It also moves. Treat everything below as a starting point to discuss with your auditor, not a conclusion.

## Where the myth breaks down

Companies already hold Bitcoin and publish audited accounts. Under IFRS, the IFRS Interpretations Committee [discussed crypto holdings in 2019](https://www.ifrs.org/projects/completed-projects/2019/holdings-of-cryptocurrencies/). It concluded that they can fall under [IAS 2 (Inventories)](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-2-inventories/) when held for sale in the ordinary course of business, and otherwise under [IAS 38 (Intangible Assets)](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-38-intangible-assets/).

In the United States, [FASB's ASU 2023-08](https://www.fasb.org/) introduced fair value measurement for certain crypto assets. Which rules apply to you depends on where you report. I point to these so you can ask the right question, not to interpret them for you.

## The auditor's path

An auditor wants to follow five steps, one after another.

<figure><img src="{{ 'images/figures/cannot-audit-or-account-1.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Five steps an auditor follows: control, existence, valuation, treatment and disclosure, with evidence prepared in advance underneath." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Control, existence, valuation, treatment, disclosure. Prepare the evidence for each before the audit starts.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Control.** Can you show that your company can sign for the keys? Usually a signed message from the wallet.
2. **Existence.** Is the balance visible on the public ledger?
3. **Valuation.** Which price source and which date will you use?
4. **Treatment.** Which accounting standard applies and why?
5. **Disclosure.** What do the notes say about risks and restrictions?

Bitcoin has one feature that helps. The ledger is public, so existence can be checked independently. That is often easier than confirming a bank balance by letter.

## What to prepare

- A list of wallet addresses and who controls them.
- A signed message proving control, produced under supervision.
- Written custody and approval procedures.
- A price-source policy, so valuation is the same every period.
- Your custody agreement, if a third party holds the keys.

The [accounting and audit guide]({{ '/guides/accounting-and-audit/' | relative_url }}) has a longer checklist.

## The bottom line

"We can't audit it" is better stated as "we haven't prepared the evidence yet."

The standards exist, the ledger is checkable, and the work is mostly documentation. Talk to your auditor early, before the first purchase, not after.
