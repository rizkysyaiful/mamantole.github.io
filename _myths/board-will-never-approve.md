## Who said "never"?

Often nobody did. The board has not been asked yet.

When an executive says "the board will never approve this," they are usually guessing what directors will fear. Sometimes they are right. Often they are describing their own worry.

## What is true in the myth

Boards are cautious on purpose. Their job is to protect the company, and a director's name is attached to every decision.

A proposal that arrives as excitement about an asset will meet resistance. It should. A board that approved things because someone was excited would not be doing its job.

## Where the myth breaks down

Boards do approve unfamiliar things every year: a new market, a currency hedge, a big acquisition. What they ask for each time is the same. Clear purpose, defined limits, named owners, and a way to stop.

So the real task is not to win an argument about Bitcoin. It is to **answer the questions before they are asked**.

## The questions directors actually ask

In my experience they come in a familiar order:

1. **Why are we doing this?** What risk does it cover, in plain words.
2. **How much?** A cap, usually as a share of reserves, and not a feeling.
3. **Where is it kept, and by whom?** Custody, controls, and what happens if someone leaves.
4. **Who decides?** Approvals, authority limits, and who can say stop.
5. **How will we report it?** Accounting treatment and a regular update to the board.

You can see these in the [policy guide]({{ '/guides/treasury-policy/' | relative_url }}) and the [board approval checklist]({{ '/guides/board-approval/' | relative_url }}).

## But wait, what if they still say no?

Then you have learned something useful, and it cost you a meeting, not a mistake.

Ask which question the "no" belongs to. Is it about size? Custody? Reputation? Each one has a different answer. A "no" with a reason can be worked on. A "no" that was never asked for cannot.

A "not yet" is also a fine outcome. It can come with a smaller first step and a date to look again.

## A better question

> What would this board need to see in writing to say yes to a small, capped first step?

That question turns an argument into a to-do list. It also treats directors as thoughtful people, which they usually are.
