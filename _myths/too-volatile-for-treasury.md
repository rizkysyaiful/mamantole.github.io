## Which risk does your board talk about?

Have you noticed which risk gets discussed in the boardroom, and which one does not?

Volatility is visible. Everyone can see a price move. Inflation on idle cash is quiet, so it rarely makes the agenda. Both are real, and neither goes away by ignoring it.

## What is true in the myth

Bitcoin has been highly volatile, with long periods of steep falls. Nobody can promise that will not happen again.

A banker would never count it as cash or a cash equivalent, and neither should a company. It belongs in a different bucket from the money that pays salaries next month.

## Where the myth breaks down

Volatility is a property of the asset. **Risk is a property of the position.**

A very small holding in a volatile asset can threaten the company less than a very large holding in a "stable" one. This is how banks and funds already think. They limit exposure, match it to a time horizon, and keep a liquidity buffer for what must be paid soon.

## Compare the two risks honestly

Idle cash carries a slow, near-certain loss of purchasing power. Bitcoin carries a large, uncertain price swing. Neither is free.

Treasury is the discipline of choosing which risks to hold, in what size, and for what reason. I think of it as a lifeboat. You do not live in it, but you want one on board in case the ship has a problem.

## What a prudent policy looks like

An illustrative example, not advice:

1. **A cap.** A small percentage of reserves, never operating cash.
2. **A gradual build.** Purchases spread over time rather than all at once.
3. **A separate buffer.** Operating liquidity kept entirely apart.
4. **A review.** Quarterly, with a pre-agreed response if the position falls below a set level or rises above the cap.

The exact numbers depend on your balance sheet, your obligations and your board. The [policy guide]({{ '/guides/treasury-policy/' | relative_url }}) shows where each of these goes.

## The bottom line

Volatility is a reason to size the position carefully, not a reason to avoid the question.

Ask three things: how much, for how long, and under what limits? If your team can answer those in writing, the board is looking at a managed risk, not a bet.
